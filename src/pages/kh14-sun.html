You have the moon's mean. It was reckoned for six o'clock — but the chapter wants it at sunset, and sunset is not at six. So the Rambam asks one question, about the sun, and answers it with at most half a degree.

Where is the sun standing?

That is the whole question. The nine bands are a ring around the year — the two equinoxes ask for nothing, and the further the sun is from them, the more the answer moves.

Why these bands, and not others

The moon covers 13° 10′ 35″ in a day, which is about 33′ an hour. So every hour that sunset falls later than six, the moon has gone another 33′ past where your six-o'clock figure put it. The Rambam's staircase is that curve, rounded to quarter-degrees.

The table he actually gives you

Eight bands, in his order, starting from the middle of דגים and running forward through the mazalos. Read down until you reach the one the sun is standing in.

KH 14:5“If the sun is located between midway in the constellation of Pisces and midway in the constellation of Aries, the moon's mean should be left without emendation. If the sun is located between midway in Aries and the beginning of Gemini, 15 minutes should be added…”
KH 14:6“The figure that remains after these additions or subtractions have been made, or when the mean was left without emendation, is the mean of the moon approximately 20 minutes after the setting of the sun… This is referred to as the mean of the moon at the time of the sighting.”
The bands are symmetric about the equinox line: 30° wide where they ask for nothing, 45° wide for a quarter degree, 60° wide for a half. Reflect the wheel across the 0°–180° axis and the pattern maps onto itself with the sign turned over — because the seasons do.

The sunset curve is not the Rambam's — it is the ordinary sunrise equation at latitude 32° with a 23½° obliquity, drawn here only to show what the staircase is approximating.
